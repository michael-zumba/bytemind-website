For many small businesses, calculating the value of trading stock at the end of the financial year can be a tedious process. However, the Income Tax Act 2007 offers significant concessions for businesses classified as "low-turnover traders". 

Here is an explanation of the official rules, translated into practical terms to help you streamline your end-of-year accounting.

### Who Qualifies as a "Low-Turnover Trader"?

According to **Section EB 13(2)** of the Income Tax Act 2007, you are considered a low-turnover trader if the total turnover (sales) of your business, combined with the turnover of any associated persons, is **$3 million or less** for the income year.

*Example:* If you own a boutique retail store with total annual sales of $250,000, your business easily qualifies as a low-turnover trader.

### The Four Valuation Methods

**Section EB 14** outlines that low-turnover traders have the flexibility to choose from four specific methods to value their closing stock (unsold goods at year-end). 

| Valuation Method (Section EB 14) | Practical Application | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cost** | You value your unsold stock at the original purchase price. | You bought 20 units for $10 each. The 5 unsold units are valued at **$10 each**. |
| **Discounted Selling Price** | You estimate cost by taking the intended selling price and subtracting your standard profit margin. | You sell the units for $20 each with a 50% margin. Value: $20 - $10 (margin) = **$10 each**. |
| **Replacement Price** | You value the stock at the current cost to purchase the identical item from your supplier today. | Original cost was $10, but the supplier's current price is $12. Value: **$12 each**. |
| **Market Selling Value** | You value the stock at the expected open-market sale price in its current condition (often wholesale value). | An obsolete item originally cost $200, but is now only worth $80 to a liquidator. Value: **$80**. |

### Two Crucial Compliance Rules

#### 1. Consistency is Required

While the legislation offers a choice of methods, you must apply your chosen method consistently. **Section EB 22** requires that your methodology aligns with generally accepted accounting practice (specifically NZIAS 2). This means you cannot arbitrarily switch methods from year to year simply to engineer a better tax outcome; any change requires a valid commercial rationale.

#### 2. The Low-Value Stock Exemption

Perhaps the most useful concession is found in **Section EB 23**. If the *total* value of your closing stock (all unsold goods combined) is **$10,000 or less**, you are exempt from performing these calculations entirely. 

You can simply treat the closing stock value as zero. This effectively allows you to fully deduct the cost of the stock in the year it was purchased, drastically simplifying your tax return.

*Example:* If your total unsold inventory at year-end costs $8,000, you can utilise the low-value rule and bypass the formal valuation methods altogether.

### Legislative Summary

For professional reference, the relevant sections of the Income Tax Act 2007 are:
- **Subpart EB**: Valuation of trading stock
- **Section EB 13(2)**: Definition of Low-Turnover Trader
- **Section EB 14**: The four valuation methods
- **Section EB 23**: The $10,000 Low-Value Stock Exemption
