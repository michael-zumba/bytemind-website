When a New Zealand business purchases services or intangible goods from an overseas supplier, the overseas supplier typically does not charge New Zealand GST. To maintain fairness for local suppliers and ensure GST is collected on consumption within New Zealand, Inland Revenue utilises the **"reverse charge" mechanism**.

Under this system, the New Zealand-resident recipient is deemed to have made a taxable supply to themselves and must pay the 15% GST directly to the government. However, this rule does not apply to everyone equally. 

### The 95% Taxable Use Threshold

The application of the reverse charge mechanism hinges on how the imported service is used. 

* **95% or more Taxable Use:** If the imported goods or services are used almost entirely (95% or more) to make taxable supplies (i.e., for core business activities), the reverse charge **does not** apply. The business simply treats the purchase as a normal expense.
* **Less than 95% Taxable Use:** If the use is less than 95% for taxable purposes—meaning a portion is for private use, or for making exempt supplies (like residential rental income)—the reverse charge **is triggered**. The recipient must account for GST on the full cost of the import.

### Practical Scenarios

Let's explore how this threshold applies in everyday business situations.

#### Example 1: The Commercial Accounting Firm (Exempt from Reverse Charge)
A commercial accounting firm in Auckland hires a US-based consultant for staff training, costing NZ$2,300. 
* **Use:** The training is used **100%** for taxable business activities.
* **Outcome:** Because 100% exceeds the 95% threshold, the firm does not apply the reverse charge. They account for the training as a standard business expense.

#### Example 2: The Freelance Photographer (Mixed Use)
Sarah, a wedding photographer, buys a specialised editing software subscription from the UK for $1,000. She uses the software 70% for client work and 30% for editing personal family photos.
* **Use:** Her taxable use is **70%**, which falls below the 95% threshold.
* **Outcome:** The reverse charge applies. Sarah must calculate 15% GST on the full $1,000 ($150) and declare this as Output Tax payable to the IRD. She may then claim back an Input Tax credit for the 70% business portion ($105). The net cost of the private use is accurately taxed.

#### Example 3: The Residential Property Owner (Exempt Supply)
Lisa owns a residential rental property and also runs a small taxable online business. She hires a Singaporean architect to draft renovation plans for the rental property for $2,000. 
* **Use:** Residential rent is an **exempt supply**, meaning her taxable use of the architectural plans is **0%**. 
* **Outcome:** Falling well below the 95% threshold, the reverse charge applies. Lisa must pay 15% GST on the $2,000 ($300) as Output Tax. Because the expense relates to an exempt supply, she cannot claim any of this back as Input Tax. 

### The Core Principle

The reverse charge acts as a "private or exempt use catch." If an imported service is used fully for taxable business purposes, the tax system trusts the standard accounting process. But the moment an import is diverted toward private enjoyment or exempt activities, the mechanism ensures the appropriate GST is levied immediately.

Understanding the 95% threshold is essential for businesses that blend commercial operations with private use or exempt income streams.
