title: "What Has Generative AI Actually Changed in Everyday Life?"
date: 2026-08-23
summary: "The brief separates measurable change from hype: wellbeing aggregates have moved slowly and remain entangled with the pandemic recovery, while the most reliable effects so far are concentrated in work and skills."
---

This research brief examines how generative AI has reshaped everyday life, comparing the pre-AI era (roughly 2015 to 2022) with the post-AI era (2023 to 2026) in a global context. The launch of ChatGPT in November 2022 marks a sharp inflection point: generative AI moved from a specialised research tool to a mass-market utility embedded in search, writing, coding, education, and daily tasks. The brief asks what changed for ordinary people across seven dimensions: quality of life, happiness, perceived freedom, stress, job security, the groups most affected, and the possibility that AI is letting non-experts perform work once reserved for specialists.

## Key findings

- Generative AI became a mass-market utility faster than any consumer application before it. ChatGPT reached roughly 100 million weekly users within a year of launch and about 800 million by October 2025. The share of US adults who had ever used it rose from 18 percent in 2023 to 44 percent in 2026 (Pew Research Center).
- Evaluative wellbeing moved modestly across the boundary. The unweighted world average of the Cantril life ladder rose from 5.38 in 2015 to 5.65 in 2025 (World Happiness Report data), but most of the rise happened in 2020 and 2021, before ChatGPT, and the post-2022 path is flat for several large economies.
- Affective wellbeing tells a different story. The global Negative Experience Index hit a record 33 in 2021 and fell to 31 by 2024, while the Positive Experience Index recovered from 69 to 72. Levels in 2024 are close to pre-pandemic norms, not a new AI-driven collapse, and the pandemic confound makes causal attribution to AI impossible.
- Daily stress fell from its pandemic peak of 41 percent (2021) to 37 percent (2024) among all adults, but workplace stress remains high: 41 percent of employees worldwide reported a lot of daily stress in 2024, and 46 percent of knowledge workers reported burnout in Microsoft's 2024 Work Trend Index.
- Job security concerns are elevated but displacement so far is modest. Only about 5.5 percent of employment in high-income countries sits in occupations highly exposed to generative AI automation (ILO), while the IMF estimates about 60 percent of jobs in advanced economies are exposed to AI in some form, roughly half benefiting and half facing negative effects.
- The effects are unevenly distributed. Young adults adopted ChatGPT far faster than older adults (58 percent of 18 to 29 year olds in the US by 2025 versus 10 percent of those 65 and over), women's employment is more exposed to automation than men's (3.7 versus 1.4 percent of employment), and urban workers in the OECD are more exposed than rural workers (32 versus 21 percent).
- Experiments consistently show large productivity gains, with the biggest gains often going to less experienced workers: 40 percent faster professional writing (Noy and Zhang 2023), 34 percent gains for novice customer support agents (Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond 2025), and 26 percent more completed tasks for software developers (Cui et al. 2026). But complex tasks tell a more cautious story: one field experiment found low-performing entrepreneurs did about 10 percent worse with AI advice.
- The empowerment question is open. Generative AI clearly lowers barriers to entry in some tasks, yet the same tools can reduce learning when used as a crutch. Students with unstructured ChatGPT access performed 48 percent better on practice problems but 17 percent worse on an exam taken without the tool (Bastani et al. 2024).
